**Variability in Clinician Expertise:** Inconsistency in medical expertise is another crucial consideration for health data. A significant gap in experience among examining doctors can affect data quality. More experienced clinicians are more likely to provide accurate diagnoses and offer the most appropriate treatments, in contrast to their less experienced colleagues. This does not only affects the accuracy of diagnoses but also influences the quality of the reported data, potentially resulting in errors such as typos or the confusion of medical concepts.

**Prescription and consumption considerations:** When considering the administration and consumption of medication, there is a gap between prescription, purchase, and actual consumption. There is no certainty that a patient consumes the medicine they are prescribed, or if they buy their medicine. It is also difficult to know if they take it and if they are truthful about their medicine intake. Therefore, relying on prescription data to indicate a patient's health condition is challenging and has to be considered when developing a predictive model. Additionally, many drugs have off-label uses, meaning they are prescribed for conditions other than what they were originally approved for. This makes interpreting drug prescription data complex and can act as noise in the modeling.

## FAIR Data Principles

With the continuously increasing amount of data being produced globally, a need exists for ensuring research data is findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable. Principles for good data, metadata, and infrastructure practices are considered important for navigating all of the available information.

[In 2016, the FAIR principles were introduced in the scientific journal Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/sdata201618). These principles represented a well-received approach to addressing data management challenges. The FAIR data principles provide a guide on how to navigate all of this data while optimizing the usage of the data.

The FAIR principles are an acronym for:

**incert figure showing the fair principles**

- **[Findable](https://www.go-fair.org/fair-principles/)**: Information about data and the metadata should be public and referenced.
- **[Accessible](https://www.go-fair.org/fair-principles/)**: Data and metadata should be possible to access.
- **[Interoperable](https://www.go-fair.org/fair-principles/)**: Use standardized data formats.
- **[Reusable](https://www.go-fair.org/fair-principles/)**: Through extensive documentation make data reusable.

FAIR data principles have been broadly adopted and are included in international, national, and local strategies for data management. Examples of initiatives where FAIR data principles are utilized include:

- [National strategi for data management baseret på FAIR-principper](https://www.deic.dk/da/data-management/nationalt-samarbejde/National-strategi-for-FAIR-data-management)
- [Aalborg University (AAU) FAIR strategy](https://www.aau.dk/om-aau/strategi/digitalisering/forskning/masterplan)
- [The Beacon Network](https://beacon-network.org/#/)
- [Genomic Data Commons (GDC) Data Portal](https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/)
- [European Genome-Phenome Archive (EGA)](https://ega-archive.org/)

For more information on FAIR principles, see [GO FAIR](https://www.go-fair.org/fair-principles/).

However, while FAIR data principles enhance data quality and accessibility, certain limitations must be considered. Complex legal systems governing data sharing often create boundaries regarding the level of detail that can be shared and frequently restrict data access. Furthermore, the extensive effort of documentation and technical requirements needed to meet FAIR standards can present a significant limitation.

Theoretical concepts and principles related to data management, such as FAIR, have now been reviewed. The next section will present concrete tools for data storage.

## Relational Databases

Digital platforms designed for the storage of data intended for modeling are known as databases. As a single dataset might be utilized for specific analyses, such a dataset is often found to be insufficient for comprehensive modeling. When multiple datasets are involved, a central storage from which necessary data can be drawn is convenient. This is where a database comes in.

Multiple datasets, referred to as tables in database terminology, can be held within a database. Operations such as customization, merging, and filtering can be performed on these tables. [Structured Query Language (SQL)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SQL) must be employed to perform data management operations. SQL is a way of communicating with the database to extract the necessary data.

As a simple example, consider a requirement where data for patients aged between 18 and 100 years must be extracted from a table named PATIENT. If data from all columns is needed, the symbol (*) is utilized. The corresponding SQL query is formulated as follows:

```SQL
SELECT *

FROM PATIENT

WHERE age BETWEEN 18 AND 100;
```

Different types of databases, and the choice of database is based on the use case and preference. A popular database type is the **relational database**, which consists of multiple tables with connections among them through foreign keys.

Relational databases are particularly valuable for dynamic risk prediction modeling as they allow researchers to efficiently query specific patient populations and integrate diverse data sources. For instance, when developing a hospital readmission risk model, a researcher could query patients based on specific diagnoses, medication histories, and demographic characteristics, rather than manually combining separate datasets.
